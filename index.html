Mihir Sharma is a designer turned HCI researcher working on haptic and multisensory interfaces, accessibility for blind and low-vision people, and calibrated trust in AI. His work asks what a person should feel, and when, so that touch helps rather than distracts.

He began with a built system tested in a climbing gym. TactileGrip, his MFA thesis, put sensing and vibration inside climbing holds so that blind climbers need not depend on a guide calling out every hold. In 2026 he turned the same question toward AI: how a visual assistant can answer through vibration, how a smartwatch should budget its mistakes, and how a system should say through touch that it is unsure. Four of these papers are under review, at ACM IUI, TEI and CHIIR and at PACM IMWUT.

He holds an MFA in User Experience Design, with Distinction, from the College for Creative Studies in Detroit, where he was advised by Predrag K. Nikolić, and a B.Des in Product Design. Before research he worked in UX and visual design, including a General Motors–sponsored autonomous-vehicle concept. He is applying to PhD programs for 2027.

Research: Haptic Human–AI Interaction

All papers are sole-authored. Papers under anonymous review are listed by title and venue; preprints, code and prototypes are available on request. Each paper discloses how AI tools, including Claude, were used, and states plainly what has and has not been tested with people.

ACM IUI 2027 Poster · under review

Can a Buzz Answer That? How Much of What Blind People Ask a Visual Assistant Fits a Vibration Vocabulary

Mihir Sharma

Visual assistants answer blind users by speech, even when the answer is yes, no, a number or “I can’t tell from this photo.” Of 8,000 held-out VizWiz questions, 15.5% could be answered with a vibration rhythm and 26.8% more with a tactile “can’t tell.” A real vision–language model (ViLT), routed by the same rules fixed before inference, gave a confident answer on 20% of the photos the crowd marked unanswerable.

Ships with Felt Answers, an offline phone prototype with identification-trial and device-check modes. The rhythms have not yet been tested with people.

ACM TEI 2027 Work in Progress · under review

Feel the Frame: Touch-Guided Camera Aiming for Blind Users of Visual AI Assistants

Mihir Sharma

A visual assistant can only answer if the photo is usable. Feel the Frame is an error-recovery policy: it detects what is wrong with a frame, lets the phone fix what it can, gives one tactile correction at a time, and gives up gracefully when a correction is not working. Five image-flaw detectors, trained and tested on 7,750 photos taken by blind people, reach held-out AUROC of .66 to .95.

Includes an Android web prototype and a four-motor sleeve design with host-tested firmware. The sleeve has not been built or worn yet.

PACM IMWUT Journal · under review

TrueTap: Budgeting How Often a Smartwatch Buzzes You by Mistake

Mihir Sharma

Activity detectors on watches nudge people when they think something is happening, and every false buzz costs attention and trust. TrueTap gives each wearer a budget for false buzzes and calibrates the detector’s threshold with a finite-sample guarantee: if new wearers resemble the calibration wearers, each goes over budget with bounded probability. On day-long wrist data from 151 adults (CAPTURE-24), pooled across tasks and budgets, it cut the share of wearers over budget from 7.6% to 3.7%, within a 9.8% bound. Tested on three more datasets, with the cases where the guarantee breaks reported.

Includes a Python library, a browser runtime and scripts that regenerate every result. Not yet run on a watch or with users.

ACM CHIIR 2027 Short Paper · under review

Material Confidence: Which Outcomes Can Tell Felt from Seen AI Confidence?

Mihir Sharma

If an AI’s confidence is felt through vibration rather than shown on screen, how would a study tell whether people use it? A re-analysis of a public dataset of 35,670 human–AI decisions, followed by a simulation-based design analysis, chooses the outcome before any data are collected. In simulated 96-person studies, reliance sensitivity detected use of the cue 99% of the time; the common appropriate-reliance measure would need about 1,820 participants.

Comes with a preregistration-ready protocol, a web pilot task and band firmware. The study has not been run.

MFA Thesis College for Creative Studies, 2024

TactileGrip: A Multi-sensory Climbing Interface for Visually Impaired Athletes

Mihir Sharma, advised by Predrag K. Nikolić

Thesis Select Award · UX Design Award nominee, 2024

Blind climbers depend on a guide calling out each hold from the ground. TactileGrip puts the guidance in the holds: a beep that quickens as the hand approaches, a vibration that confirms the grip, and forms that point to the next hold. After 20 interviews with blind and low-vision climbers, guides and coaches, all 10 blindfolded expert climbers in a gym study completed more of the route than with verbal guidance alone (p = .002).

SenseShift: Adaptive AI‑to‑Haptic Communication for Blind and Low-Vision People

Mihir Sharma

Scene-describing assistants tend to talk about whatever is nearest. SenseShift decides what deserves a blind or low-vision person’s attention and says it through six vibration pods on the forearm, keeping speech free for what needs words. In 10,500 simulated walks, the attention policy cut messages about distractors from 58.9% (nearest-object baseline) to 6.2%.

Three studies with blind and low-vision adults are fully specified, with study apps, band firmware and an analysis pipeline that accepts only real session data. No sessions have run yet.

DetroTune: Sound, Space and Cultural Memory in VR

Mihir Sharma

Exhibited at Ars Electronica Festival and Detroit Month of Design

A Unity VR environment that teaches the cultural history of Detroit techno through spatial sound and motion. In a mixed-methods study (N = 40), participants showed higher memory retention and engagement than with video-based learning.
