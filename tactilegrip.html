TactileGrip: Real-time Sensor-Embedded Climbing Holds for Non-Visual Route Navigation

Mihir Sharma, Predrag K. Nikolić (thesis advisor)

Thesis Select Award · UX Design Award nominee, New Talent, 2024

Summary

Blind and low-vision climbers depend on a sighted guide calling directions from the ground, often as clock positions (“hold at two o’clock”). That voice has to carry over gym noise, be understood under load, and describe holds the guide can barely see from below, and every move waits for the next instruction.

TactileGrip moves hold-level guidance into the hold itself. The idea is that a climber without sight does not need a description of the wall. They need the wall to answer three questions at each move: Is my hand close? Have I got it? Where next? Each question gets its own signal, and all sensing and feedback run on an Arduino Nano inside the hold, with no phone, wearable or person in the loop during the climb.

  • QuestionCan feedback built into the holds replace a guide’s hold-level verbal directions?
  • ContributionA working real-time system inside climbing holds, and two design findings about how touch should carry guidance.
  • Evidence20 interviews; a blindfolded workshop with 10 expert climbers; a preliminary in-gym evaluation with the same 10 (10 of 10 got further, p = .002).
  • LimitationAll evaluated climbers were sighted and blindfolded. The study with blind and low-vision climbers is specified, not yet run.

Who took part, and how

Nine months, in three phases: formative research, design and build, and evaluation. Three evaluation studies were interleaved with the build. The validation study with blind and low-vision climbers follows this period.

Blindfolded sighted climbers were used only to study what vision contributes to climbing and to test the system’s mechanism, not as a stand-in for blind climbers. Simulating disability can misrepresent disabled experience, so claims about benefit for blind and low-vision climbers are reserved for the validation study.

System

Proximity stage
An HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor reads hand distance every 50 ms. The hold is silent beyond 50 cm; within it, a beep speeds up as the hand closes and is fastest at 5 cm. The range matches one hand’s reach, so climbers on nearby routes do not trigger it.
Contact stage
When the hand lands, the beep stops and a coin vibration motor fires one pulse. Sound means “almost there” and vibration means “you have it”, so the climber never has to judge state from the loudness of one signal.
Directional form
Hold C has grooves for the index and middle fingers and leans toward the next hold, so the hand is oriented before the climber decides where to move. Chosen from three printed candidates and refined over three printed iterations in PLA.
Star holds
An optical pulse sensor in the grip surface gates a pre-recorded message played through a DFPlayer Mini, such as “Hurray! You’ve reached checkpoint 3!”. The gate rejects contact from a rope, a bag or a passer-by’s hand.
Companion app
Voice-first: every screen was tested with blindfolded users by voice alone before any visual design. Climbers book a slot and meet their co-climber’s profile; afterwards a leaderboard is read aloud.

Results and design findings

Preliminary in-gym evaluation

Each of the 10 blindfolded climbers climbed the familiar route twice: Verbal, with the partner calling directions as in the workshop, and TactileGrip, with the partner present for safety only. Sessions were video-recorded.

MeasureVerbalTactileGrip
Climbers who completed more of the route–10 / 10
Directional instructions from the partnerEvery moveNone
Partner’s roleNavigation and safetySafety only

Exact two-sided sign test on the direction of change: p = 2·2−10 = .002; proportion improved 10/10, 95% Clopper–Pearson CI [.69, 1.00]. These records show the direction of the effect, not its size. Climbers, partners and gym staff understood the system from the feedback alone and asked for no changes; climbers called moving along the directional holds “natural” rather than “guided”.

Two design findings

Direction is felt, not decoded. Of three printed forms, a protruding arrow (Hold A) concentrated pressure on the palm and read as “push here”, and a gap-slot grip (Hold B) hurt the fingertips within seconds. With Hold C, every participant found the intended direction by touch alone. The shape that looked most like an arrow was the most confusing to the hand: direction should be built into the grip a form affords, not drawn on its surface.

Approach and arrival need separate channels. An early prototype vibrated only on contact. Because climbing moves are physical and hard to undo, that signal came after the decision, sometimes on the wrong hold, and participants called it “a surprise, not a guide”. Adding the proximity beep before contact produced the two-stage design that participants anticipated.

A third finding came from the app: the minimal booking screen was praised as efficient, but participants would not book without knowing who they would climb with. Minimal is not the same as accessible when an action carries stakes.

Limitations and next study

  • The completed evaluations used sighted climbers who were blindfolded. They show that the feedback can replace verbal hold-level guidance when visual feedback is removed, but not how much it helps blind and low-vision climbers, who bring non-visual strategies the experts lack. The benefit could be smaller, or larger.
  • The evaluation used a familiar route and one session per climber, so order and practice effects cannot be ruled out.
  • The workshop and evaluation involved the same ten climbers, and the results establish the direction of the effect, not its magnitude.

Planned validation with blind and low-vision climbers

35 climbers with prior top-rope experience (up to 40 enrolled), recruited through adaptive-climbing and para-climbing networks. Within-subject: Verbal and TactileGrip on two unfamiliar routes of matched grade, fully counterbalanced across four orders, after a 10-minute familiarisation.

Primary measure: share of route holds reached. Secondary: full completions, freezes, partner directions, time per hold, placement errors, workload (NASA-TLX) and confidence, plus an interview. Two coders score the video. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with 35 people has 80% power for a medium effect (dz = 0.50). Every hold is bench-tested first, and the study will be preregistered before recruitment.
